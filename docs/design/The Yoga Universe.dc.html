THE SOURCE TEXT

The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali

196 short aphorisms, compiled somewhere between 200 BCE and 400 CE, that define what yoga actually is. Three of them mention posture.

Patanjali did not invent yoga and probably did not write the Sutras alone. He compiled an existing oral tradition into four short books — the padas — in a compressed style meant to be memorised and then unpacked by a teacher. This is why the text feels terse to the point of unhelpfulness on first reading, and why almost nobody reads it without a commentary.

Its second verse is the whole definition, and it says nothing about the body: yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ — yoga is the stilling of the movements of the mind. Everything else in the text is method or consequence.

The four books

BOOK I · SAMADHI PADA · 51 VERSES

What yoga is, what the mind does instead, and the states of absorption available to someone who stops it. Written as if for a reader already practising.

BOOK II · SADHANA PADA · 55 VERSES

The practical book: the causes of suffering, and the eight limbs. This is where posture appears, in three verses, described only as a steady and comfortable seat.

BOOK III · VIBHUTI PADA · 56 VERSES

Concentration, meditation and absorption — and a long catalogue of resulting powers, which Patanjali then warns are obstacles rather than achievements.

BOOK IV · KAIVALYA PADA · 34 VERSES

The philosophical close: the nature of mind and matter, and liberation as the end of identification rather than the gaining of anything.

The eight limbs

Ashtanga — literally eight limbs. The word names this structure, long before it named a sequence of postures.

1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 OUTWARD CONDUCT INWARD
1 · Yama How you treat others. Non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing, restraint, non-grasping.
2 · Niyama How you treat yourself. Cleanliness, contentment, discipline, self-study, surrender.
3 · Asana A seat that is steady and comfortable. That is the entire instruction given.
4 · Pranayama Regulation of the breath, and through it the regulation of attention.
5 · Pratyahara Withdrawing the senses from what they habitually chase.
6 · Dharana Concentration — holding attention on one thing, deliberately.
7 · Dhyana Meditation — when that holding becomes continuous and no longer needs effort.
8 · Samadhi Absorption, in which the separation between observer and observed drops away.

WHY IT MATTERS TO A TUESDAY CLASS

If posture is one of eight limbs and gets three verses out of 196, then a practice built entirely on it is not wrong — it is simply small. Most teachers in this index will tell you the same thing in their own words.

Where to start reading

Read a translation with commentary, not a bare one. Bare translations of a deliberately compressed text produce confident misreadings.

Read Book II first, then Book I. Book III's list of powers derails most first readings, and Book IV means little until the rest has settled.

PLACES · UTTARAKHAND, INDIA

Rishikesh

Where the Ganges leaves the Himalaya and the yoga tourism industry begins. A pilgrimage town of 100,000 that trains a large share of the world's yoga teachers.

A short history

Rishikesh has been a place of retreat for far longer than it has been a destination. Its name — from rishi, seer — reflects a tradition of ascetics settling where the Ganges emerges from the mountains, and the town appears in Puranic literature as a stop on the pilgrimage route north to Badrinath and Kedarnath. For most of its history it was a staging post: pilgrims rested, bathed at the ghats, and walked on.

The modern ashram town took shape in the twentieth century. Swami Sivananda founded the Divine Life Society here in 1936 and, unusually for the time, taught and published in English — which is much of why Rishikesh rather than Haridwar or Varanasi became the place foreign students came to. His students went on to found lineages of their own, and by the 1950s the west bank held a cluster of teaching ashrams.

In February 1968 the Beatles arrived at Maharishi Mahesh Yogi's Transcendental Meditation ashram and stayed several weeks, writing much of what became the White Album. The visit turned a regional pilgrimage town into a global one almost overnight. The ashram itself was abandoned in the 1990s and its graffiti-covered meditation cells now sit inside the Rajaji reserve, open to visitors.

What followed is harder to romanticise. Rishikesh now hosts hundreds of teacher trainings, and the quality range is enormous — from ashrams teaching in six-month terms to four-week certificate mills. The town also carries an alcohol and meat ban, a March yoga festival that draws tens of thousands, and a whitewater rafting industry sharing the same river. All of it coexists, not always comfortably.

Come in October to March. April and May are hot, and the monsoon from June closes much of the river.

GETTING THERE

Jolly Grant airport (DED), 20km — an hour from Delhi.

Haridwar Junction, 25km, is the practical railhead.

Six hours by road from Delhi, longer in the monsoon.

What is on offer

Teacher training

The town's main export. 200hr and 300hr courses, mostly residential, concentrated in Tapovan and Upper Swarg Ashram. Check accreditation and how many hours are actually taught, not advertised.

Ashram stays

Traditional residential practice with a fixed daily schedule, shared rooms and a 5am start. Parmarth Niketan and the Sivananda ashram take long-stay students; several accept walk-ins.

Drop-in classes

Hatha, Ashtanga and Iyengar all over the east bank, often on rooftops, usually 200–500 rupees. Quality varies by teacher rather than by venue — ask who is teaching, not where.

Silent & meditation retreats

Vipassana courses, silent sits and shorter three-day retreats, mostly on the quieter hillsides above the town rather than in the centre.

Ayurveda & panchakarma

Consultations, massage and full panchakarma programmes of one to three weeks. Look for a BAMS-qualified practitioner; the title is a real degree and easy to check.

Ganga Aarti

The evening fire ceremony at Triveni Ghat and Parmarth Niketan. Free, daily at sunset, and the one thing worth doing on your first evening.

Sound, kirtan & philosophy

Sanskrit and sutra study, chanting groups and kirtan most evenings. The philosophy courses are the most under-attended and often the best value in town.

The river, and walking

Rafting from September to June, beaches upstream, and the walk up to Neelkanth Mahadev — traditionally done on foot, and still the best day out of town.
